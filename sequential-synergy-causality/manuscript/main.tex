\title[Anytime-Valid Sequential Testing of Group Synergy]{Anytime-Valid Sequential Testing of Group Synergy: Interventional and Predictive Formulations, with an Application to Financial Markets}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Quang-Vinh} \sur{Dang}}\email{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{British University Vietnam}, \orgaddress{\city{Hung Yen}, \postcode{160000}, \country{Vietnam}}}

\abstract{Does a \emph{group} of variables act on an outcome jointly, beyond the sum
of its members' separate contributions, and can this be decided \emph{as data
arrive}, stopping when the evidence is conclusive? Existing synergy measures
(sufficient-cause interaction indices, joint-effect estimators, information-theoretic
decompositions) are fixed-sample. We introduce ANTE, an anytime-valid sequential test
of a super-additive synergy null built on testing by betting: a bounded interaction score is
accumulated into a nonnegative capital process (an e-process), and Ville's inequality gives
time-uniform Type-I error control under optional stopping. In the \emph{interventional} part
(known randomised design) the null is sequential, $\theta_t\le0$ for all $t$, so stationarity
is not required. In the \emph{predictive} part (ANTE-SG, for time series) the null compares two
learned forecasters, an additive and a joint one, through a truncated-loss score whose bound is
known before each observation; this makes the validity proof exact and replaces a clipping
argument that we show to be invalid in general. We prove validity for the forecaster-comparison
null, power one and a high-probability detection-time bound for an oracle bet, a $k$-way
extension, and bounds on the size of synergy that can be excluded, and we state sufficient
conditions under which a rejection can be read as an $A\times B$ interaction. Simulations show
that the procedure holds its level under continuous monitoring, including heavy-tailed,
correlated and nonstationary nulls, but that its power on real financial noise is low: an injected lagged interaction carrying $40\%$ of the variance of a daily return series is detected in fewer than half of real triples. Type-I control for population-level nulls is checked by simulation and by
placebo calibration on real data, not proved. In a fully specified search of $27{,}027$ hypotheses over financial
panels we find no statistically significant super-additive predictive group effect in daily and
weekly returns and volatility, where the evidence matches a placebo null; for stress indicators and
hourly crypto data the test rejects a true placebo null in $10$--$40\%$ of hypotheses, and we do not interpret
rejections there as synergy.}

\keywords{anytime-valid inference, e-processes, testing by betting, synergy, group synergy, predictive synergy, Granger causality}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Some effects require a coalition. Two drugs that are inert alone can be lethal
together; two genes silent in isolation can jointly switch on a phenotype; two
market shocks, each absorbed on its own, can compound into a crisis. The common
structure is \emph{synergy}: the joint action of a set of variables exceeds the sum
of their individual actions. Distinguishing ``you need
\emph{both} $A$ and $B$'' from ``$A$ helps and $B$ helps, separately''
is a recurring scientific question, and a subtle one, because it is a
statement about an \emph{interaction}, not about any single variable.

The question has been formalised at least three times, in largely separate
literatures. In epidemiology, Rothman's sufficient-component-cause model
\citep{rothman1976} defines synergy as two factors being component causes of the
same causal ``pie'', measured by the additive-scale interaction contrast and its
relative-excess-risk index \citep{hosmer1992,vanderweele2014}, with a complete
counterfactual axiomatisation for arbitrarily many binary causes due to
\citet{vanderweele2012} and, more recently, a multi-factor generalized synergy
index \citep{latorre2026}. In statistics, a parallel programme estimates the
effect of a \emph{joint} intervention $\mathrm{do}(\{A,B,\dots\})$ by fusing
experiments \citep{roquero2023} or identifying the joint law of potential
outcomes \citep{wu2025}. In information theory, Partial Information Decomposition
\citep{williams2010} and its dynamical descendants SURD
\citep{surd2024} and PEID \citep{peid2026} split the information that sources
carry about a target into redundant, unique, and \emph{synergistic} parts.

These methods share one limitation that this paper addresses: they are
\textbf{fixed-sample}. Each returns a point estimate, and at best a confidence
interval, at a sample size chosen \emph{before} looking at the data. None
supports the mode of inference that streaming data demand: watch the
evidence accumulate and stop when it is decisive, without paying a
multiplicity penalty for having looked. A practitioner who applies an unadjusted
fixed-sample synergy test repeatedly (``peeking'') generally loses its nominal
Type-I error; we show below that for the repeated Wald procedure the error
rises from a nominal $5\%$ to about $50\%$.

The machinery for continuous monitoring now exists. Testing by
betting \citep{shafer2021}, e-processes and safe anytime-valid inference
\citep{ramdas2023,grunwald2024}, and confidence sequences \citep{howard2021}
provide tests whose validity holds \emph{simultaneously at all sample sizes} and
under \emph{any} data-dependent stopping rule. A sceptic bets against the null; if
the null is true the sceptic's capital is a nonnegative supermartingale and cannot
grow, so large capital is evidence against the null, quantified by Ville's inequality
\citep{ville1939}. This programme has produced anytime-valid tests for means
\citep{waudbysmith2024}, off-policy and average treatment effects
\citep{waudbysmith2024ope}, comparisons of sequential forecasters
\citep{choe2024}, conditional independence \citep{grunwald2024ci} and kernelised
independence \citep{podkopaev2023}. To the best of our knowledge, it has not
been applied to an interaction or synergy contrast.

\paragraph{What is, and is not, claimed.} We keep four notions apart throughout.
\emph{Interaction} is a property of a contrast ($\mu_{11}-\mu_{10}-\mu_{01}+\mu_{00}$ in
the interventional part, a difference of predictive gains in the predictive part).
\emph{Synergy} is a positive (super-additive) interaction. \emph{Causal} synergy needs
an interventional or counterfactual definition and a design that identifies it; the
interventional part supplies both under a known randomised design. \emph{Predictive}
synergy, tested in the time-series part, is a statement about out-of-sample
forecasting: it is Granger-type predictability and is not, by itself, evidence of a
structural interaction. We therefore speak of ``predictive group synergy'' for the
time-series procedure and reserve ``causal'' for the interventional one.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A sequential test of synergy with an exact validity proof, in two
formulations.} (i) Interventional ANTE: under a known, possibly adaptive, randomised
design we build a bounded interaction score that is centred at the conditional
contrast $\theta_t$; the one-sided null $\theta_t\le0$ for all $t$ gives a nonnegative
supermartingale (Theorem~\ref{thm:validity}) and, via Ville's inequality, time-uniform
Type-I control (Corollary~\ref{cor:anytime}). The null is sequential, so
nonstationarity of the contrast is allowed. (ii) Predictive ANTE-SG: the null compares
two learned forecasters, and a truncated-loss score normalised by a bound that is
known before each observation gives an exactly valid e-process
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid}). This replaces a clipping construction that we show
to fail in general (Example~\ref{ex:clipfail}). Part~A is a specialisation to a contrast vector of the anytime-valid average-effect e-processes of \citet{waudbysmith2024ope}; its novelty is the target (an interaction) and the sequential null, not the betting machinery.

\item \textbf{Power theory, stated for what it covers.} For an oracle fixed bet we prove
power one (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}) and a non-asymptotic, high-probability
detection-time bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:latency}); the bound extends to a mixture
over a fixed grid of bets (Proposition~\ref{prop:mixture}). For the implemented plug-in
bet we prove validity only, and evaluate power empirically.

\item \textbf{Groups, exclusion and multiplicity.} A $k$-way extension contrasts an
additive with an all-interaction forecaster (Corollary~\ref{cor:group}); an
\emph{exclusion} e-process against persistent synergy (Proposition~\ref{prop:excl}) and a time-uniform upper bound on the running average of the conditional synergy (Proposition~\ref{prop:avg}) quantify what can be ruled out; e-Bonferroni and e-BH control family-wise error and FDR
across many hypotheses.

\item \textbf{A precise isolation analysis.} We show when the add-versus-joint contrast
isolates an $A\times B$ interaction from joint dependence and own-nonlinearity
(Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}), prove that the four-model contrast $\Syn_4$ does not (it can be negative or positive under purely additive
mechanisms with correlated sources), and give counterexamples showing that
additivity alone is not enough.

\item \textbf{An empirical programme with an explicit search space and honest calibration.} Simulations cover
heavy-tailed, correlated, nonstationary and nonlinear alternatives, with Monte Carlo
uncertainty throughout. A formally specified financial search over daily, weekly and
hourly panels, three target types and four lag structures ($27{,}027$ hypotheses) finds no family-wise detection in daily and weekly returns and volatility, and the real-data evidence there coincides with a placebo null;
the placebo also shows that the test is \emph{not} calibrated for binary stress indicators or hourly crypto series, so rejections there are not interpreted. We report the power of the test on real noise,
which is low, and we show that physical turbulence data, a natural positive control, fail a placebo check.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work; Section~\ref{sec:setup} fixes notation and
the two nulls; Section~\ref{sec:partA} develops the interventional test and
Section~\ref{sec:partB} the predictive test, including a table of what is proved and
what is only evaluated empirically (Table~\ref{tab:guarantees}).
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations. Proofs are in the appendices.

\section{Related work and positioning}\label{sec:related}

Group causality, the notion that a set of causes acts on an effect jointly beyond the sum
of its members, has been studied in four research traditions. Three formalise
\emph{what synergy is} (epidemiological sufficient-cause interaction, statistical
joint-effect estimation, and information-theoretic decomposition); the fourth supplies the
inferential machinery we import (game-theoretic sequential testing).

\subsection{Sufficient-cause and additive-scale interaction}\label{subsec:suff}

Rothman's \emph{sufficient-component-cause} model \citep{rothman1976} defines synergy as
two factors being component causes of a common sufficient cause. For two binary causes
with counterfactual cell means $\mu_{ab}=\E[C(a,b)]$, this is captured on the
additive (risk-difference) scale by the interaction contrast
$\theta=\mu_{11}-\mu_{10}-\mu_{01}+\mu_{00}$; the \emph{relative excess risk due to
interaction} (RERI) was placed on a firm inferential footing by \citet{hosmer1992} and
systematised by \citet{vanderweele2014}. \citet{vanderweele2012} give empirical and
counterfactual conditions for sufficient-cause and \emph{singular} interaction among an
arbitrary number of dichotomous exposures; this is the batch null our interventional test
most directly generalises. Mechanistic interaction has its own measures, such as the
peril-ratio index PRISM \citep{lee2013} and its gene--gene extension \citep{lin2018}, and
\citet{latorre2026} generalise Rothman's synergy index to many factors. All of these are
fixed-sample.

\subsection{Joint effects, causal aggregation, and multi-cause identification}\label{subsec:joint}

A second tradition asks what the effect of intervening on several causes at once is.
\citet{roquero2023} develop \emph{causal aggregation} for the effect of a joint
intervention from experiments that each manipulate few variables; \citet{wu2025} study
nonparametric identification of the joint distribution of potential outcomes; and deep
structural-equation approaches \citep{balgi2024} estimate interventional and
counterfactual distributions. These are effect-estimation methods with a fixed design;
none provides a sequentially valid test of a synergy contrast.

\subsection{Information-theoretic synergy and higher-order interactions}\label{subsec:info}

Interaction information \citep{mcgill1954} was the first attempt to isolate the
information that three or more variables share beyond their pairwise relations.
\emph{Partial Information Decomposition} (PID; \citealp{williams2010}) splits the
information that two sources carry about a target into redundant, unique and synergistic
atoms; \citet{lyu2026} show that the lattice becomes inconsistent for four or more
variables. The O-information \citep{rosas2019} indicates whether a system is net-synergistic
or net-redundant. For dynamical systems, SURD \citep{surd2024} decomposes
transfer-entropy-style causality into synergistic, unique and redundant components, and
PEID \citep{peid2026} provides an interventional analogue of PID in which synergy is
$EI(A,B\!\to\!C)-\sum_i EI(T^{(i)}\!\to\!C)$. These quantities answer a different question
from the one tested here: they measure how much information is jointly carried, under
their own definition of synergy, and they are \emph{estimators} rather than tests, with
no null distribution, calibrated error rate or stopping rule. We compare against them in
Section~\ref{subsec:exp-benchmark} as complementary tools, not as competitors on a common
estimand.

\subsection{Synergy and causality in time series and finance}\label{subsec:tsfin}

Directed predictability is formalised by Granger causality \citep{granger1969,geweke1982}
and, information-theoretically, by transfer entropy \citep{schreiber2000}; the two coincide
for Gaussian variables \citep{barnett2009}. \citet{stramaglia2014} show that pairwise
Granger analysis misses synergy and that conditioning reveals it. In finance,
\citet{scagliarini2020} compute PID synergy over triplets of market indices, and the
connectedness literature measures system-wide spillovers through variance decompositions
\citep{diebold2014} and through pairwise Granger-causality networks \citep{billio2012}.
These works measure synergy or connectedness in sample, at a fixed sample size, without an
out-of-sample, error-controlled test.

\subsection{Testing by betting and anytime-valid inference}\label{subsec:betting}

The inferential machinery we import is game-theoretic statistics \citep{ramdas2023}: Ville's
inequality \citep{ville1939}, testing by betting \citep{shafer2021}, safe testing
\citep{grunwald2024}, the hedged-capital betting strategies for bounded means of
\citet{waudbysmith2024}, time-uniform confidence sequences \citep{howard2021} and universal
inference \citep{wasserman2020}. Continuous-monitoring validity distinguishes these from
classical tests, whose guarantees degrade under repeated looking
\citep{johari2022}; the classical remedy is a group-sequential design with alpha spending
\citep{pocock1977,obrienfleming1979,landemets1983}, which we use as a comparator.
E-values can be merged under arbitrary dependence \citep{vovk2021} and support FDR control
via e-BH \citep{wang2022}. Two lines are close to our predictive part: sequential
comparison of forecasters \citep{choe2024}, whose null we adopt (Section~\ref{sec:partB}),
and anytime-valid conditional-independence and kernelised independence tests
\citep{grunwald2024ci,podkopaev2023}, which could supply a causal or nonlinear route that we
leave to future work. The distributional Granger test of \citet{jha2026} is a sequential
test of a \emph{single} source.

\subsection{The gap, and the claim}\label{subsec:gap}

We distinguish five properties: (P1) anytime-valid inference for an interaction contrast;
(P2) anytime-valid \emph{causal} inference; (P3) sequential inference for effects of
\emph{groups}; (P4) predictive synergy; (P5) information-theoretic synergy.
Table~\ref{tab:gap} records which of them each strand addresses. A focused search (terms:
\emph{anytime-valid interaction test}, \emph{e-process causal interaction},
\emph{sequential test synergy}, \emph{confidence sequence interaction effect},
\emph{test martingale synergistic}) did not return a sequential, anytime-valid test whose
null is that the joint effect of a set of variables equals the sum of its parts. A negative
search result cannot be exhaustive, and the literature on e-values and online testing is
growing quickly. We therefore make the following restricted claim: \emph{to the best of our
knowledge, ANTE is the first framework we are aware of that combines (P1) and (P3), with an
interventional (P2, under a known design) and a predictive (P4) instantiation}. We do not
claim to be the first anytime-valid test of any synergy-like quantity, and we do not
address (P5) beyond comparing with it.

\section{Setup: two formulations of the synergy null}\label{sec:setup}

We develop the null on two scales that share the idea \emph{joint equals sum of
parts} but differ in what is being estimated and what is assumed. They are kept
separate in the theory (Sections~\ref{sec:partA} and~\ref{sec:partB}) because they
support different interpretations.

\subsection{Interventional synergy}\label{subsec:setup-int}

Observations arrive as a stream with unit $t$ carrying $(X_t,A_t,B_t,C_t)$, where
$C_t\in[0,1]$ is a bounded outcome, $A_t,B_t\in\{0,1\}$ are binary causes, and $X_t$ are
optional covariates. Let $\F_t=\sigma\big((X_s,A_s,B_s,C_s)_{s\le t}\big)$ and
$\mathcal H_t=\F_{t-1}\vee\sigma(X_t)$. Let $C_t(a,b)$ be the potential outcome
under $\mathrm{do}(A=a,B=b)$.

\begin{assumption}[Known sequential design]\label{ass:design}
For every $t$: (i) \emph{consistency without carry-over}, $C_t=C_t(A_t,B_t)$, so potential outcomes at time $t$ depend only on the causes assigned at time $t$ (no interference between periods); (ii) \emph{known sequential
randomisation}, $\Prob(A_t=a,B_t=b\mid\mathcal H_t,\{C_t(\cdot,\cdot)\})=\pi_t(a,b)$ with
$\pi_t$ known and $\mathcal H_t$-measurable (adaptive designs are allowed); (iii)
\emph{positivity}, $\pi_t(a,b)\ge\pi_{\min}>0$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{definition}[Conditional additive interaction contrast]\label{def:theta}
\[
\theta_t(X_t)=\sum_{a,b}c(a,b)\,\E\big[C_t(a,b)\mid\mathcal H_t\big],\qquad
\theta_t=\E\big[\theta_t(X_t)\mid\F_{t-1}\big],
\]
with $c(a,b)=(-1)^{(1-a)+(1-b)}$, so that $c=+1$ on the cells $(1,1),(0,0)$ and $-1$ on
$(1,0),(0,1)$. If the cell means do not depend on $t$, $\theta_t\equiv
\mu_{11}-\mu_{10}-\mu_{01}+\mu_{00}$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{sequential} nulls are
\begin{align*}
H_0^{\le}&:\ \theta_t\le0\ \text{for all }t \quad\text{(one-sided: no super-additive synergy)},\\
H_0^{=}&:\ \theta_t=0\ \text{for all }t \quad\text{(two-sided)}.
\end{align*}
They allow the contrast to vary over time: a fixed-parameter null $\theta=0$ is the special
case $\theta_t\equiv\theta$. The scientific question of super-additive synergy is
inherently one-sided, and $H_0^{\le}$ is the null we use in the applications; the
two-sided null is needed only to detect antagonism. For example, an XOR mechanism has $\theta=-2$ on this additive scale (the cell means of the concordant cells are zero and those of the discordant cells one), so it is a \emph{negative} additive interaction; information-theoretic decompositions call XOR ``pure synergy'' because neither source alone is informative, which is a different notion.

\subsection{Predictive group synergy for time series}\label{subsec:setup-granger}

Observational streams admit no intervention, so we use an out-of-sample predictive
definition in the spirit of Granger \citep{granger1969}. Let $Y$ be a target, $A,B$ candidate
sources, $Z$ an optional conditioning set (for example a market factor), and $p$ the lag
order. For $S\subseteq\{A,B\}$ let $L(S)$ be the expected one-step loss of the best
(linear least-squares) predictor of $Y_t$ from the $\{Y,Z\}$-past augmented by the lagged histories of $S$, and
$\Delta(S)=L(\varnothing)-L(S)$ its predictive gain. Two contrasts are natural:
\begin{align}
\Syn_{4}(A,B\!\to\!Y)&=\Delta(\{A,B\})-\Delta(\{A\})-\Delta(\{B\}), \label{eq:syn4}\\
\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}(A,B\!\to\!Y)&=L(\mathrm{add})-L(\mathrm{joint}), \label{eq:synaj}
\end{align}
where ``add'' is the best predictor from the base set plus \emph{both} sources' own
terms (linear and squared lags) and ``joint'' additionally contains the cross-products
$A_{t-\ell}B_{t-\ell}$. $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}$ is the \emph{add-versus-joint} contrast.
$\Syn_4$ is the more obvious interaction of predictive gains; we show in
Section~\ref{subsec:isolation} that it is contaminated by redundancy between $A$ and
$B$ and should not be used to isolate interaction. In practice the test is run on the
\emph{learned} online forecasters, whose null we state in Section~\ref{subsec:sgnull}.

Both $\Syn_4$ and $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}$ are predictive quantities. A positive value says that
the joint past of $(A,B)$ forecasts $Y$ better than an additive combination; it does not by
itself identify a structural interaction. In particular, for Gaussian variables
Granger causality equals transfer entropy \citep{barnett2009}, so $\Syn$ can be read as an
interaction of conditional transfer entropies, but that reading is predictive, not causal.

\section{Part A: interventional ANTE}\label{sec:partA}

\subsection{A bounded score, centred at the conditional contrast}\label{subsec:score}

Under Assumption~\ref{ass:design} define the inverse-probability-weighted interaction score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
\psi_t \;=\; c(A_t,B_t)\,\frac{C_t}{\pi_t(A_t,B_t)} .
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Conditional unbiasedness and boundedness]\label{lem:unbiased}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:design}, $\E[\psi_t\mid\mathcal H_t]=\theta_t(X_t)$ and hence
$\E[\psi_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\theta_t$. Moreover $|\psi_t|\le c_\psi:=1/\pi_{\min}$; for a
balanced $2\times2$ design $\psi_t\in[-4,4]$.
\end{lemma}

The bound, not any variance or tail assumption, is what makes the betting argument
exact. An augmented (AIPW) score is discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:aipw}.

\subsection{The betting e-process and its validity}\label{subsec:eprocess}

Fix a level $\alpha\in(0,1)$. Start with unit capital and bet a \emph{predictable} fraction
$\lambda_t$, that is, an $\F_{t-1}$-measurable function of the past:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capital}
E_t\;=\;\prod_{s=1}^{t}\bigl(1+\lambda_s\psi_s\bigr),\qquad
\lambda_s\in\Big[0,\frac{\gamma}{c_\psi}\Big]\ \ (\text{one-sided}),\ \ \gamma<1 .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Validity, nonstationary null]\label{thm:validity}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:design}:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] (one-sided) if $\lambda_s\in[0,\gamma/c_\psi]$, then under $H_0^{\le}$ the process
$(E_t)$ is a nonnegative supermartingale with $E_0=1$;
\item[(b)] (two-sided) let $E_t^{+}$ and $E_t^{-}$ be built from predictable
$\lambda_s^{+}\in[0,\gamma/c_\psi]$ and $\lambda_s^{-}\in[-\gamma/c_\psi,0]$. Under $H_0^{=}$
the average $\tfrac12(E_t^{+}+E_t^{-})$ is a nonnegative martingale with mean $1$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Anytime-valid Type-I control]\label{cor:anytime}
In either case, $\Prob\bigl(\exists\,t\ge1:\;E_t\ge 1/\alpha\bigr)\le\alpha$.
The guarantee holds uniformly over all stopping times: one may monitor after every
observation, stop early, or continue indefinitely. $E_\tau$ is an e-value at every stopping
time $\tau$, and $1/\max_{s\le t}E_s\wedge 1$ is an anytime-valid $p$-value.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Scope of the two-sided process]\label{rem:twosided}
The two-sided average is valid under $H_0^{=}$ but \emph{not} under $H_0^{\le}$: if
$\theta_t<0$ the leg with $\lambda\le0$ has positive drift. We therefore use the one-sided
process for the question ``is there super-additive synergy?''.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why peeking a fixed-sample test fails]\label{rem:peeking}
A fixed-$n$ test controls $\Prob(\text{reject at } n)$ for one pre-committed $n$. Consulting
an unadjusted fixed-sample test after every observation reports
$\Prob(\exists t:\text{reject at }t)$, which in general exceeds the nominal level and
grows with the number of looks. Corollary~\ref{cor:anytime} is the statement such a
procedure lacks. We quantify the inflation for a repeated Wald test in
Section~\ref{subsec:exp-validity}; that procedure is included to illustrate the
optional-stopping failure, not as a competing anytime-valid method.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The choice of bet.} Any predictable $\lambda_t$ is valid; a \emph{good} one grows
capital fast under the alternative. We use a truncated plug-in Kelly fraction from the
running moments of $\psi$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grapa}
\lambda_t=\min\Bigl\{\max\Bigl(\frac{\hat\mu_{t-1}}{\hat v_{t-1}},0\Bigr),\frac{\gamma}{c_\psi}\Bigr\},
\qquad
\hat\mu_{t-1}=\tfrac{1}{t-1}\!\sum_{s<t}\psi_s,\quad
\hat v_{t-1}=\tfrac{1}{t-1}\!\sum_{s<t}\psi_s^2,
\end{equation}
the aGRAPA-type strategy of \citet{waudbysmith2024} specialised to the interaction score,
with $\lambda_t=0$ during a warm-up of $t_0$ steps and whenever $\hat v_{t-1}=0$. The warm-up, the cap $\gamma$ and the
moment estimators are part of the definition of the e-process, not of the algorithm only,
and are fixed before data are seen.

\subsection{Power and detection time (oracle bet)}\label{subsec:power}

Validity without power is vacuous, so we state what can be proved about power, and for
which bet. The results below hold for an \emph{oracle fixed bet} $\lambda$ (or a fixed
grid); validity of arbitrary predictable bets, power of the oracle bet, and the behaviour
of the implemented plug-in bet are three different things, and we prove only the first two.

\begin{assumption}[Persistent alternative]\label{ass:alt}
$|\psi_t|\le c$ and $\E[\psi_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\ge\delta>0$ for all $t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:log}
For $\gamma\in[0,1)$ and $x\ge-\gamma$, $\log(1+x)\ge x-C_\gamma x^2$ with
$C_\gamma=\frac{-\log(1-\gamma)-\gamma}{\gamma^2}\le\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)}$ (and $C_0=\tfrac12$).
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Power one for the oracle bet]\label{thm:consistency}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:alt}, fix $\lambda\in(0,\gamma/c]$ and put $\bar\gamma=\lambda c$.
Then almost surely
\[
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac1t\log E_t\;\ge\;g_0(\lambda):=\lambda\delta-C_{\bar\gamma}\lambda^2c^2
\;\ge\;\lambda\delta-\frac{\lambda^2c^2}{2(1-\gamma)} .
\]
If $g_0(\lambda)>0$ then $E_t\to\infty$ a.s., so the anytime-valid test rejects with
probability one. The cap-based Kelly choice $\lambda^\star=\delta(1-\gamma)/c^2$, admissible when $\delta(1-\gamma)\le\gamma c$ (always if $\gamma\ge\tfrac12$ and $\delta\le c$), gives
$g_0\ge\delta^2(1-\gamma)/(2c^2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Non-asymptotic detection-time bound]\label{prop:latency}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:alt}, for a fixed $\lambda$ with $g_0=g_0(\lambda)>0$ and
$\ell_\lambda=\log\frac{1+\lambda c}{1-\lambda c}$, the rejection time
$\tau_\alpha=\inf\{t:E_t\ge1/\alpha\}$ satisfies, for every $t>\log(1/\alpha)/g_0$,
\[
\Prob(\tau_\alpha>t)\;\le\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{2\,(t\,g_0-\log(1/\alpha))^2}{t\,\ell_\lambda^2}\Bigr).
\]
Consequently, with probability at least $1-\beta$,
$\tau_\alpha\le t_\beta=\max\bigl\{2\log(1/\alpha)/g_0,\ 2\ell_\lambda^2\log(1/\beta)/g_0^2\bigr\}$.
At the cap-based Kelly bet this is of order $\log(1/\alpha)/((1-\gamma)\delta^2)$ for
the first term and $\log(1/\beta)/\delta^2$ for the second.
\end{proposition}

This is a high-probability bound, not an almost-sure bound and not a bound on
$\E\tau_\alpha$: detection time scales as $\delta^{-2}$ in the effect and only
logarithmically in $1/\alpha$.

\begin{proposition}[Mixture over a fixed grid of bets]\label{prop:mixture}
Let $0<\lambda_1<\dots<\lambda_K\le\gamma/c$ be fixed before the data are seen and
$E^{\mathrm{mix}}_t=K^{-1}\sum_j\prod_{s\le t}(1+\lambda_j\psi_s)$. Then
$E^{\mathrm{mix}}$ is a nonnegative supermartingale under $H_0^{\le}$, and
$\log E^{\mathrm{mix}}_t\ge\max_j\log E_t(\lambda_j)-\log K$. Hence
Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} and Proposition~\ref{prop:latency} hold for the best grid
point with $\log(1/\alpha)$ replaced by $\log(K/\alpha)$. On the geometric grid
$\lambda_j=(\gamma/c)2^{-(j-1)}$, $j=1,\dots,K$, with $K$ large enough that $\lambda_K\le\lambda^\star$, the rate loss relative to the quadratic lower bound $\lambda\delta-C_\gamma\lambda^2c^2$ at its maximiser is at most a factor $4/3$.
\end{proposition}

For the implemented plug-in bet \eqref{eq:grapa} we prove validity only; its power and
detection time are evaluated in simulation (Section~\ref{subsec:exp-validity}). Regret
guarantees for related betting strategies exist \citep{waudbysmith2024}, but we do not
claim them for the specific truncated plug-in used here.

\subsection{Augmented (AIPW) scores}\label{subsec:aipw}

Let $\phi_t=\sum_{a,b}c(a,b)\bigl[\tfrac{\indic\{A_t=a,B_t=b\}}{\pi_t(a,b)}
\bigl(C_t-\hat\mu_{ab,t}(X_t)\bigr)+\hat\mu_{ab,t}(X_t)\bigr]$ with outcome models
$\hat\mu_{ab,t}\in[0,1]$ fitted on past data only (so $\F_{t-1}$-measurable). Under a
\emph{known} design $\E[\phi_t\mid\mathcal H_t]=\theta_t(X_t)$ and $|\phi_t|\le1/\pi_{\min}+2$,
so Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} holds for $\phi_t$ with $c_\psi$ replaced by $1/\pi_{\min}+2$, whatever the quality of
$\hat\mu$ (Appendix~\ref{secA:proofs}). With an \emph{estimated} propensity score the
centring error is second-order but non-zero, so finite-sample validity is not guaranteed;
we present that case as an extension and leave its finite-sample theory to future work.

\section{Part B: predictive ANTE-SG for time series}\label{sec:partB}

\subsection{The null is a comparison of two forecasters}\label{subsec:sgnull}

At each $t$ two forecasts of $Y_t$ are made from information available before $Y_t$ is
observed: $p^{\mathrm{add}}_t$ from the additive model and $p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t$ from the
joint model. Both are produced by online forgetting recursive-least-squares (RLS)
learners \citep{ljung1999} updated only after each outcome. Let $\ell(\cdot)$ be a loss and
let the per-step score be $s_t=\ell(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{add}}_t)-\ell(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t)$; $s_t>0$
means the joint forecaster did better. The null is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preq}
H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}:\qquad \E\big[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}\big]\le0\quad\text{for all }t ,
\end{equation}
a prequential (forecaster-comparison) null in the sense of \citet{dawid1984} and
\citet{choe2024}. It is a statement about the \emph{realised, learned} forecasters, and
requires no stationarity and no correct specification of the data-generating process.

\begin{remark}[What $H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$ is not]\label{rem:notpop}
$H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$ is not the population statement $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=0$. If
$\mu_t=\E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]$, $e_a=p^{\mathrm{add}}_t-\mu_t$ and $d=p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t-p^{\mathrm{add}}_t$, then for the squared loss
$\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=-2de_a-d^2$, which is positive if and only if $de_a<-d^2/2$, that is, when the joint refit moves the forecast in the direction opposite to the additive model's current estimation error by less than $2|e_a|$. Averaged over estimation noise it
is negative (the out-of-sample penalty of the larger nested model,
\citealp{clarkwest2007}), but that is an average, not a conditional statement, and
forgetting learners move slowly, so favourable stretches can persist. A rejection therefore
means: \emph{the joint forecaster had a persistently positive conditional predictive
advantage over the additive forecaster}. Non-rejection and exclusion statements
(Section~\ref{subsec:excl}) are statements about these forecasters; they bear on population
synergy only under the conditions of Section~\ref{subsec:isolation}, which are checked in
simulation but cannot be verified in data.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Filtration and predictability.} Let $\F_t$ be the natural filtration of the raw
observations (of the whole panel, when many hypotheses are scanned). Every ingredient used
to form $E_t$ is $\F_{t-1}$-measurable at the time the bet is placed: (i) the standardisation
of each series uses only running moments of past values; (ii) the features are fixed functions
of lagged standardised values; (iii) the RLS state is a fixed function of past
features and outcomes, and the code predicts before updating, so $p^k_t=\langle w^k_{t-1},\phi^k_t\rangle$;
(iv) the normaliser $b_t$ below is a function of the predictions; (v) the bet $\lambda_t$ is a function of
$u_1,\dots,u_{t-1}$. All tuning constants (forgetting factor, ridge, lag order, truncation
level, bet cap, warm-up, feature maps and any random-feature frequencies) are fixed before
any data are seen; selecting any of them using the series under test voids the guarantee.
If features contain contemporaneous values $(A_t,B_t,Z_t)$, the null and $b_t$ are conditioned on
$\mathcal G_t=\F_{t-1}\vee\sigma(A_t,B_t,Z_t)$ and validity follows by the tower property;
such a test concerns contemporaneous interaction and not Granger-type prediction.

\subsection{A bounded score with a predictable bound}\label{subsec:score-sg}

A natural construction scales the raw squared-error score by a predictable standard
deviation and clips it, arguing that clipping and positive scaling preserve the
sign of the conditional mean. That argument is false in general, and we do not use it.

\begin{example}[Clipping can reverse the sign of the conditional mean]\label{ex:clipfail}
Let $s$ take the value $-20$ with probability $0.1$ and $+1.5$ with probability $0.9$, with
$\hat\sigma=1$ and clip level $b=5$. Then $\E s=-0.65\le0$ but
$\E\,\clip(s/\hat\sigma,-b,b)=0.1(-5)+0.9(1.5)=+0.85>0$. A bettor that wagers a fixed
fraction on i.i.d.\ draws grows its capital at a positive exponential rate, so the clipped
test rejects a true raw null with probability one. A data-dependent scale does not
repair this once the tail is heavier (Appendix~\ref{secA:proofs}); we reproduce the
failure in simulation in Section~\ref{subsec:exp-validity}.
\end{example}

Clipping is valid for the null stated \emph{on the clipped score}, and for the raw null under
conditional symmetry (Appendix~\ref{secA:proofs}); neither is what a user of the test wants.
We replace clipping by a \emph{truncated loss} and a \emph{predictable bound}. Let
$\ell_c(e)=\min(e^2,c^2)$ for a truncation level $c>0$ (in units of the predictably
standardised target). Because $\ell_c$ is $2c$-Lipschitz, for any two predictable
forecasts and every outcome
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bound}
\bigl|\ell_c(Y_t-p^{1}_t)-\ell_c(Y_t-p^{2}_t)\bigr|\;\le\;2c\,|p^{1}_t-p^{2}_t|.
\end{equation}
The bound does not use nesting and is known \emph{before} $Y_t$ is observed. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sg-score}
\begin{gathered}
s_t=\ell_c(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{add}}_t)-\ell_c(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t),\qquad
b_t=2c\,|p^{\mathrm{add}}_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t|,\\
u_t=\frac{s_t}{b_t}\,\indic\{b_t>\varepsilon\}\in[-1,1],
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with a fixed threshold $\varepsilon\ge0$ (if $b_t=0$ the two forecasts coincide and
$s_t=0$). Dividing by the predictable positive $b_t$ preserves the sign of the conditional
mean \emph{exactly}: $\E[u_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\indic\{b_t>\varepsilon\}/b_t$.
No clipping and no distributional assumption are needed.

\begin{theorem}[Exact validity of ANTE-SG]\label{thm:sgvalid}
Let $\lambda_t\in[0,\gamma]$ be predictable, $\gamma<1$, and
$E_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1+\lambda_su_s)$. Under $H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$, $(E_t)$ is a nonnegative
supermartingale with $E_0=1$. Hence $\Prob(\exists t:E_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$, and $E_\tau$ is
an e-value for every stopping time $\tau$. The same holds for the capped process
$\min\{E_{t-1}(1+\lambda_tu_t),C\}$ for any constant $C\ge1$ (the implementation uses
$C=10^{15}$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[$k$-way groups]\label{cor:group}
For a group $\mathcal G=\{A_1,\dots,A_k\}$ let $p^{\mathrm{add}}$ come from the feature map
$\Phi^{\mathrm{add}}$ (each member's own linear and squared lags) and $p^{\mathrm{joint}}$ from
$\Phi^{\mathrm{joint}}$ which adds all same-lag products of order $2,\dots,k$. Then
Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid} holds verbatim under the group null
$\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\le0$, with $b_t=2c|p^{\mathrm{add}}_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t|$.
\end{corollary}

The same construction applies to the four-model score
$s_t=\ell_c^A+\ell_c^B-\ell_c^{\mathrm{joint}}-\ell_c^{\mathrm{base}}$ with
$b_t=2c(|p^A-p^{\mathrm{joint}}|+|p^B-p^{\mathrm{base}}|)$; we keep it only as a diagnostic.

\subsection{What the add-versus-joint contrast isolates}\label{subsec:isolation}

Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid} is valid for $H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$ whatever the data. What
\emph{rejection means} depends on the population structure, and it is easy to over-state it. Work in the least-squares projection framework: second-order
stationarity, finite fourth moments, untruncated squared loss, population-optimal linear
predictors on the feature blocks of Section~\ref{subsec:setup-granger}.

\begin{proposition}[Isolation, corrected]\label{prop:isolation}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}\ge0$, and $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=0$ if and only if every cross-product
$A_{t-\ell}B_{t-\ell}$ is orthogonal to the residual of the additive projection.
\item[(b)] $\Syn_4=\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}-R$, where the redundancy term $R$ vanishes when the two
sources' feature blocks are orthogonal after conditioning on the base, but otherwise can have
either sign. For jointly Gaussian $(A,B)$ with correlation $\rho$ and $Y=A\pm B+\varepsilon$ one
has $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=0$ while $\Syn_4=-2\rho(1+\rho)$ for the plus sign and $+2\rho(1-\rho)$ for the minus sign.
\item[(c)] Under an additive mechanism $Y_t=m(W_t)+f(\mathcal A_t)+h(\mathcal B_t)+\varepsilon_t$ (with $\E[\varepsilon_t\mid W_t,\mathcal A_t,\mathcal B_t]=0$ and finite fourth moments),
$\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=0$ in each of the following cases: (S1) $m(W)$ lies in the span of the base features and $f$ and $h$ lie in the own-feature
spans (linear plus quadratic lags), with arbitrary dependence between $A$ and $B$; (S2) the
base, the $A$-past and the $B$-past are mutually independent ($f,h$ arbitrary; then $R=0$ too);
(S3) one lag, $(A,B)$ jointly Gaussian and independent of the base ($f,h$ arbitrary, cubic terms included).
\item[(d)] Without such conditions $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}>0$ is possible under a mechanism that
involves a single source. For example, with $A,V\sim N(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon$ independent, $B=A^2+\tau V$ and $Y=A^3+\varepsilon$ gives
$\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=36/(6+\tau^2)$; a fully Gaussian two-lag example ($p=2$, $A_t,V_t$ i.i.d.\ $N(0,1)$), $B_t=A_{t-1}+vV_t$ and
$Y_t=A_{t-1}A_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t$, gives $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=1/(1+v^2)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}

Hence an e-process rejection of $H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$ can be read as evidence of an irreducible
$A\times B$ interaction only under conditions such as (c); when sources are dependent and the
mechanism may be nonlinear outside the feature span, it is evidence that the joint
forecaster beats the additive one. We therefore do not claim that the test ``provably isolates'' interaction from joint dependence; what holds is Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}
and the simulations of Section~\ref{subsec:exp-corr}.

\subsection{Estimation noise and negative evidence}\label{subsec:cw}

The joint forecaster estimates more coefficients than the additive one. With an effective window of
$n_{\mathrm{eff}}=(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ observations (about $400$ for a forgetting factor $\rho=0.995$)
this estimation noise does not vanish, and it gives the score a negative average drift
even when the population contrast is zero or small and positive. Validity is unaffected
(a more negative drift only slackens the supermartingale inequality), but it follows that a
small final capital $E_t<1$ carries no evidential weight \emph{for} the null: e-values below one are not
evidence of absence. We report a sensitivity analysis over $\rho$ and the Clark--West
adjusted score $s_t+(p^{\mathrm{add}}_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t)^2$ \citep{clarkwest2007}
as a diagnostic of the noise floor. The adjusted score is \emph{not} guaranteed to be
centred, so it is not used for inference.

\subsection{Exclusion: bounding the size of synergy}\label{subsec:excl}

A rejection cannot be used the other way round, so absence statements need a separate instrument. Two are provided: an e-process against a
persistent alternative, and a time-uniform upper bound on the running average of the conditional synergy.

\paragraph{Units.} The score is a difference of \emph{truncated} squared losses of a predictably standardised target, so its unit is a per-step gain in truncated squared error
per unit target variance. Only about $2\%$ of the observations of the series used below lie beyond three predictable standard deviations, but they carry $34$--$63\%$ of the sum of squares
(Section~\ref{subsec:exp-fin}), so a gain of $\delta$ in these units is not comparable to a gain in untruncated out-of-sample $R^2$ by a comparable factor; we call it ``loss units''.

\paragraph{Persistent alternative.} For $\delta>0$ let
$H_\delta:\ \E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\ge\delta$ for all monitored $t$.
With $w_t(\delta)=(\delta-s_t)/(b_t+\delta)\in(-1,1]$ and predictable $\lambda_t\in[0,\gamma]$, put $K_t(\delta)=\prod_{s\le t}(1+\lambda_sw_s(\delta))$.

\begin{proposition}[Exclusion e-process]\label{prop:excl}
Under $H_\delta$, $K(\delta)$ is a nonnegative supermartingale, so $\Prob(\exists t:K_t(\delta)\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$. For a finite grid $G$ of values of $\delta$ with per-$\delta$ bets,
rejecting every $\delta$ with $\max_tK_t(\delta)\ge|G|/\alpha$ makes the probability of rejecting any true $H_\delta$ in the grid at most $\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

Rejecting $H_\delta$ excludes \emph{persistent} synergy of at least $\delta$ at every monitored time; it does not exclude synergy confined to a regime or an average synergy of that size. Because
$\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\le b_t$, $H_\delta$ forces $b_t\ge\delta$, so the process wins mechanically on steps where the two learners nearly agree. On real data, with learners that agree to within $0.0005/2c$ at
some steps, this made the persistent exclusion uninformative (nearly every hypothesis excluded the smallest $\delta$ on the grid), and we therefore do not use it for the finance conclusions.

\paragraph{Running-average synergy.} Let $m_s=\E[s_s\mid\F_{s-1}]$, $S_t=\sum_{s=t_1}^{t}s_s$ and $V_t=\sum_{s=t_1}^{t}b_s^2$ over the monitored steps $s\ge t_1$ ($t_1$ fixed in advance, after the warm-up).

\begin{proposition}[Time-uniform bound on average synergy]\label{prop:avg}
Let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_K>0$ be a grid fixed before the data are seen. With probability at least $1-\alpha$, simultaneously for all $t\ge t_1$ and all $j$,
\[
\frac1{t-t_1+1}\sum_{s=t_1}^{t}m_s\;\le\;\frac{S_t}{t-t_1+1}+\frac{\lambda_jV_t/2+\log(K/\alpha)/\lambda_j}{t-t_1+1}
\]
(and the symmetric lower bound). The inequality holds without any null assumption; it bounds the average of the conditional means of the score, in loss units, for the
realised forecasters. For $m$ hypotheses replace $\alpha$ by $\alpha/m$.
\end{proposition}

This is the bound reported in Section~\ref{subsec:exp-fin}. It is valid whatever the data are, but it concerns the conditional mean of the score under the learned forecasters and shares with the other results the
reading of Remark~\ref{rem:notpop}.

\subsection{Many hypotheses}\label{subsec:multi}

For $m$ hypotheses with e-processes $E^{(i)}$, each a supermartingale under its null, rejecting
$H_i$ when $\sup_tE^{(i)}_t\ge m/\alpha$ (e-Bonferroni) controls the family-wise error rate at
level $\alpha$ under arbitrary dependence. e-BH \citep{wang2022} controls the FDR at level
$\alpha|I_0|/m$ when fed e-values $E^{(i)}_\tau$ at a stopping time $\tau$ (never the running
maximum, which is not an e-value); if $\tau$ depends on all streams, each null is conditioned on the
whole-panel past. The price is a threshold of $m/\alpha$: $\log_{10}(m/\alpha)=4.1$ for $m=660$ and
$4.9$ for $m=4400$, against $1.3$ at $m=1$, so detection takes roughly $3$--$4$ times longer, or the detectable
$\delta$ is $1.8$--$2$ times larger. Zero family-wise detections are therefore weak evidence of
absence on their own, and absence statements use the time-uniform bound on average synergy of Proposition~\ref{prop:avg}, with the same multiplicity correction.

\subsection{Algorithm and a summary of guarantees}\label{subsec:algo}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Interventional ANTE (one-sided)}\label{algo:ante}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require stream $(X_t,A_t,B_t,C_t)$; known design $\pi_t$; level $\alpha$; cap $\gamma<1$; warm-up $t_0$
\State $E\gets1$; running $\hat\mu,\hat v\gets0$
\For{$t=1,2,\dots$}
  \State $\psi_t\gets c(A_t,B_t)\,C_t/\pi_t(A_t,B_t)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:psi}}
  \State $\lambda_t\gets \min\{\max(\hat\mu/\hat v,0),\gamma/c_\psi\}$ \textbf{if} $t>t_0$ \textbf{else} $0$ \Comment{predictable}
  \State $E\gets E(1+\lambda_t\,\psi_t)$
  \If{$E\ge1/\alpha$} \Return \textbf{reject $H_0^{\le}$} at time $t$ \EndIf
  \State update $\hat\mu,\hat v$ with $\psi_t$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{ANTE-SG (predictive group synergy, streaming)}\label{algo:antesg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require panel $Y,A,B$, conditioner $Z$; lag $p$; level $\alpha$; forgetting $\rho$; truncation $c$; cap $\gamma$; warm-up $t_0$
\State standardise each series predictably; init two forgetting-RLS learners (add, joint); $E\gets1$
\For{$t=p+1,\dots,T$}
  \State form $p^{\mathrm{add}}_t,\,p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t$ from information up to $t-1$ \Comment{predict before updating}
  \State $b_t\gets2c|p^{\mathrm{add}}_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t|$;\ observe $Y_t$;\ $s_t\gets\ell_c(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{add}}_t)-\ell_c(Y_t-p^{\mathrm{joint}}_t)$;\ $u_t\gets s_t/b_t$ if $b_t>\varepsilon$ else $0$
  \State $\lambda_t\gets\min\{\max(\hat\mu_{t-1}/\hat v_{t-1},0),\gamma\}$ \textbf{if} $t>t_0$ \textbf{else} $0$
  \State $E\gets E\,(1+\lambda_t u_t)$
  \If{$E\ge1/\alpha$} \Return \textbf{joint forecaster advantage detected} at $t$ \EndIf
  \State update both learners and the running moments of $u$ with $(\text{features}_t,Y_t)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

The programme answers five questions in turn. Does the theory hold, including under
nulls that stress it (\S\ref{subsec:exp-validity})? What does the add-versus-joint score isolate when
sources are correlated or the mechanism is nonlinear (\S\ref{subsec:exp-corr})? How does the
test compare with batch estimators and with a classical group-sequential design
(\S\ref{subsec:exp-benchmark})? Does it find interactions that are present in real data
(\S\ref{subsec:exp-pv})? And what does a fully specified search of financial data show, and how
much can it exclude (\S\ref{subsec:exp-fin})? Every number below can be regenerated from the scripts
in \texttt{experiments/revision/}.

\paragraph{Defaults.} Level $\alpha=0.05$; the test rejects when the running maximum of the e-process
reaches $1/\alpha=20$ (equivalent to the first-crossing rule). ANTE-SG uses lag order $p=1$ unless
stated, a forgetting factor $\rho=0.995$ (effective window about $400$ steps), ridge $10^{-2}$, truncation
level $c=3$ in units of the predictably standardised target, bet cap $\gamma=0.9$, warm-up $t_0=50$,
and feature winsorisation at $\pm5$ for real data. Real series are standardised with running moments of the
past (30-observation warm-up). The conditioner is the market factor
(SPY, or its volatility/stress transform for those targets) and the same transform is applied to every
series of a panel. Rates are reported with Wilson 95\% intervals \citep{wilson1927}. In figures and
tables ``original test'' denotes the clipped-score procedure (Example~\ref{ex:clipfail}), ``four-model''
the contrast of Eq.~\eqref{eq:syn4}, and ``ANTE-SG'' the add-versus-joint test of
Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid}. Capital is capped at $10^{15}$, so $\log_{10}E=15$ means ``at or above the cap''.

\subsection{Validity, stress tests and power}\label{subsec:exp-validity}

\paragraph{Interventional test under continuous monitoring.} On $2000$ null streams of length $4000$
(balanced $2\times2$ design, $\theta=0$) the one-sided e-process rejects in $0.019$ of streams
(95\% CI $[0.014,0.026]$). A Wald test recomputed after every observation (the optional-stopping
failure of Remark~\ref{rem:peeking}) rejects in $0.513$ $[0.492,0.535]$. A classical group-sequential
design, a Lan--DeMets-spent Pocock or O'Brien--Fleming test with $20$ equally spaced looks, rejects in $0.051$
$[0.042,0.061]$ for both boundaries, as designed (boundary constants by Monte Carlo of Brownian look
statistics with $400{,}000$ paths, total crossing probability $0.0500$). Under an AND mechanism
($\theta=0.35$) all procedures reject in every stream. The median detection sample size is $390$ for the one-sided
ANTE process ($472$ two-sided), $400$ for the Pocock design and $1000$ for O'Brien--Fleming; the peeking Wald test
stops at $102$ but with the inflated error just described. A group-sequential design is therefore the right
classical comparator and attains similar detection times \emph{when its look schedule is fixed in advance};
the e-process requires no such schedule and remains valid for any stopping rule
(Fig.~\ref{fig:interv}). Under the nonstationary nulls of Section~\ref{subsec:setup-int}
(contrast switching between $-0.2$ and $0$ in four blocks, and drifting linearly from $-0.3$ to $0$) the one-sided
process rejects in $0.001$ of streams (CIs up to $0.004$), whereas the two-sided process, whose null is false by construction
at times when $\theta_t\neq0$, rejects in $0.583$ and $0.955$ as it should (Remark~\ref{rem:twosided}). The interventional and predictive Type-I figures in this section come from different designs ($2000$ streams of length $4000$ versus $250$
streams of length $3000$ with $15$ looks) and are not directly comparable.

\paragraph{The clipping argument fails; the new score does not.} We reproduce Example~\ref{ex:clipfail} by
simulation. With $\hat\sigma=1$ and clip level $5$, a plug-in bettor on i.i.d.\ draws from the example law
($\E s=-0.65$) reaches $E\ge20$ in $0.978$ of $2000$ streams ($[0.970,0.983]$, median $\log_{10}$ of the running
maximum $8.9$), although the raw null is true; with a data-dependent scale (the expanding root mean square) the same
law is not harmful ($0.004$), which is why such a defect can go unseen in simulations.
For the new score no such construction exists for the prequential null, because Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid} does not use the shape of the law of $s_t$.
Table~\ref{tab:stress} and Fig.~\ref{fig:stress} report ANTE-SG, the four-model score and the original test on twelve
VAR nulls ($T=2500$, $200$ replications each) built around the structure in the proof: heavy-tailed skewed
innovations, GARCH volatility clustering, source correlation up to $0.99$, cubic additive terms outside the feature span
with correlated sources, a sign break of the additive coefficients at $T/2$, own-nonlinearity, persistent ($\mathrm{AR}=0.9$)
sources, and short ($0.98$) and long ($0.999$) forgetting. ANTE-SG's false-rejection rate is at most $0.010$ ($2/200$) in every
scenario. The four-model score rejects $0.170$ $[0.124,0.228]$ of nulls under persistent sources (N10)
and $0.160$ $[0.110,0.227]$ at a realised source correlation of $0.20$ (Section~\ref{subsec:exp-corr}), although its raw mean score is negative in those cells (about $-0.004$ and $-0.02$). The rejections therefore do not
come from redundancy, which Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}(b) predicts to make this score negative here; they indicate that the prequential null of the four-model score fails in finite samples for the learned forecasters (Remark~\ref{rem:notpop}; we have not isolated the cause), another reason to prefer the add-versus-joint contrast.
As an additional check against spurious-regression behaviour, independent AR(1) series with coefficient up to $0.999$ ($100$
replications each) give ANTE-SG false-rejection rates of at most $0.01$ ($1/100$ at $\varphi=0.97$, none otherwise) and none for the original test.
The limits of this evidence appear in Section~\ref{subsec:exp-fin} and the discussion: smooth, near-deterministic signals break it.

\paragraph{Power and detection time.} For a bilinear mechanism $Y_t=cA_{t-1}B_{t-1}+0.2A_{t-1}+0.3Z_t+\varepsilon_t$
(realised source correlation $0.36$, $T=2500$, $500$ replications per $c$; the figures at family-wise thresholds below use $150$ other replications and agree within the intervals), ANTE-SG's power is $0.07$ $[0.05,0.09]$ at $c=0.1$, $0.78$ $[0.74,0.81]$ at $c=0.2$ and $\ge0.998$ for
$c\ge0.3$, with false-rejection $0.000$ at $c=0$; the original test reaches only $0.34$ at $c=0.2$ and $0.60$--$0.69$ for $c\ge0.3$.

(the effect grows $4.5$-fold while the median detection time falls $9.6$-fold; the proposition is for an oracle bet and a different score, and we do not claim the bound is tight). For the original test, power dips at the largest $c$ ($0.69\to0.63$, within Monte Carlo error: the intervals overlap) and latency rises ($1095\to1473$, outside Monte Carlo error because that test's scale estimator degrades);

fixed bet; the plug-in bet is evaluated here only empirically.

\subsection{Correlated sources and nonlinear alternatives}\label{subsec:exp-corr}

A natural question is what happens when sources are strongly correlated while the mechanism is additive. We vary the
source correlation (realised correlation $0.20$ to $0.99$) for an additive mechanism and for an interaction of strength $0.9$ ($150$ replications per cell).
ANTE-SG's false-rejection rate is $0.000$ in all seven cells ($[0,0.025]$). The mean raw four-model score decreases from $-0.02$ at correlation $0.2$ to $-1.04$
at $0.99$ on the additive mechanism, as the redundancy term predicts, whereas the add-versus-joint score stays between $-0.04$ and $-0.007$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:corr}). The
cost of isolating the interaction is power when the two sources become nearly collinear: ANTE-SG's power against the interaction is $1.00$ up to correlation $0.68$, $0.97$ $[0.92,0.99]$ at $0.84$,
$0.19$ $[0.13,0.26]$ at $0.92$, $0.007$ at $0.96$ and $0$ at $0.99$. When $A\approx B$ the cross-product $AB$ is nearly collinear with the own-quadratic features
$A^2,B^2$ and the interaction is not identifiable; this is a property of the problem, not an artefact, and it bounds what any add-versus-joint test can claim for near-collinear assets
(for example for two equity sector funds). The four-model score loses power sooner ($0.013$ at correlation $0.68$) because its redundancy term turns negative, and the original test is the least powerful
throughout ($0.73$ at correlation $0.2$, $0.31$ at $0.44$, $0$ from $0.68$).

\paragraph{The test is only as nonparametric as its predictor class.} The dictionary used above (own linear and squared lags plus
cross-products) detects a bilinear and a bounded \emph{tanh$\times$tanh} or \emph{sin} interaction ($100$ replications, power $1.00$ each) because their projections onto the cross-product are strong, but it
fails on $Y_t=0.9A_{t-1}^2B_{t-1}+\dots$, an interaction outside its span: power $0.03$ $[0.01,0.08]$ at $T=2500$ and again at $T=5000$.
Replacing the dictionary by a random-Fourier-feature map \citep{rahimirecht2007} (fifteen features per source and fifteen for the pair, with fixed seed-independent frequencies) detects it with power $1.00$ $[0.96,1.00]$
at both sample sizes, while its false-rejection rate on the additive null remains $0.00$ $[0,0.04]$. The framework (the betting construction and Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid}) accommodates any predictable forecaster pair; the
RLS-on-polynomials instantiation is one choice, and we describe it as a ``model-based prequential time-series implementation of an anytime-valid testing framework'' rather than as a nonparametric test.

\subsection{Benchmark against batch synergy estimators}\label{subsec:exp-benchmark}

SURD \citep{surd2024}, PEID \citep{peid2026}, Williams--Beer PID \citep{williams2010}, Gaussian interaction information \citep{mcgill1954} and
O-information \citep{rosas2019} are \emph{estimators} of information-theoretic synergy, a different estimand from the predictive contrast tested here. We therefore report the comparison as one of
complementary tools. Each baseline was first checked against its own source: our PEID implementation returns $1.00$ for XOR and $0.19$ for AND (published value $0.189$), PID and SURD agree on XOR ($1.00$) and
give $0.50$ on AND, and all return $\approx0$ on a redundant copy and on independent noise. Settings (not tuned per method): SURD with six equal-mass bins per variable on the lagged triple $(A_{t-1},B_{t-1},Y_t)$ through
the authors' released code; PEID with polynomial-lifted affine-Gaussian mutual information under an independent maximum-entropy intervention; interaction information the Gaussian co-information of the same triple; for the monitoring comparison a permutation test
on interaction information with $50$ permutations at $15$ equally spaced looks. Detection AUC uses $100$ replications of each of three DGPs (additive, own-nonlinear, synergistic; $T=3000$, per-replication seeds).

On detection of the DGP built for the add-versus-joint contrast, batch SURD and PEID are as accurate as ANTE-SG (AUC $1.00$ each; the original test, $0.992$, and interaction information, $0.865$, are below), so ANTE-SG does not dominate
the batch estimators and is not claimed to; they answer a different question and return a value without an error guarantee under repeated looks, which is what the permutation-based entry measures. The group-sequential tests
applied to the raw per-step synergy score are valid at their $15$ pre-planned looks and powerful, with nominal errors below $0.05$ because their variance is estimated and the raw score is conservative; they are the appropriate
classical reference, and ANTE-SG's advantage over them is not accuracy at a pre-planned look but validity at \emph{any} stopping time. Gaussian PID and the other decompositions assign positive synergy to
$Y=A+B$ because their estimand is ``information about $Y$ available only from $A$ and $B$ jointly''; we do not describe this as an error. The SURD/PEID rows for additive mechanisms are therefore a difference in estimand,
not a failure. The distributional Granger test of \citet{jha2026} is a sequential test of a single source and cannot express a group.

\subsection{Positive controls on real data}\label{subsec:exp-pv}

\paragraph{Contemporaneous portfolio variance (explicit target).} The realised variance of a two-asset equal-weight portfolio, $RV_t=(0.5r_{A,t}+0.5r_{B,t})^2$, is a deterministic function of the
two contemporaneous returns, so its super-additivity ($2w_Aw_B\sigma_{AB}$) is mechanical. It is a check that the contemporaneous variant can recover an interaction that is known to be there, not an
example of group causality: we do not call it causal synergy, and we separate three notions (contemporaneous statistical interaction; predictive group synergy; causal/interventional synergy) throughout.
On $14$ asset pairs (listed in the repository), $RV_t$ as target, contemporaneous features and no conditioner, ANTE-SG rejects in all $14$ at level $0.05$ and in $13$ at the family-wise threshold $14/\alpha$ (the remaining pair, XLF/XLI, has correlation $0.86$).
The lagged counterpart is not significant for any pair at the family-wise threshold ($1/14$ at the raw level). Two features of this pattern need explanation, and both follow from the target definition. The near-collinear
within-equity pairs have the weakest signal ($\log_{10}E$ of $5.4$ to $8.9$ for XLK/XLI, XLE/XLF, XLE/XLB; $1.7$ for XLF/XLI) because the regression of the cross product $r_Ar_B$ on the own features $(1,r_A,r_B,r_A^2,r_B^2)$ has $R^2=0.955$--$0.995$
for them, so the cross term is almost fully explained by the own-quadratic terms already in the additive model; for the cross-asset pairs that $R^2$ is $0.03$--$0.45$. Equity--bond pairs have negative covariance (for example XLF/TLT, corr $-0.26$) and are detected strongly:
the test measures the \emph{predictive gain} from the cross term, which does not depend on the sign of the covariance, so negative covariance does not make a pair ``sub-additive'' in this sense. Capital saturates the $10^{15}$ cap for most cross-asset pairs.

\paragraph{Semi-synthetic power on real noise.} To measure power on genuine financial noise, we inject a known lagged interaction into real series: $Y'_t=z(C)_t+\beta\,z(A)_{t-1}z(B)_{t-1}$ for $40$ random real triples of each of two datasets
(daily stock returns conditioned on SPY; daily ETF volatility conditioned on $|$SPY$|$), re-standardised predictably. At $\beta=0$ (Type-I on real noise) the single-hypothesis rejection rate is $0.025$ and $0.050$ ($40$ triples each), and no triple reaches a family-wise threshold.
Power is low even for large injected terms (Fig.~\ref{fig:semisyn}): at $\beta=0.5$, an injected term that carries on average $40\%$ ($35\%$) of the variance of $Y'$, power is $0.43$ ($0.25$) at the single-hypothesis threshold, $0.28$ ($0.13$) at the $m=660$ family-wise threshold and
$0.23$ ($0.13$) at $m=4400$; $0.8$ power is not reached on the grid. A natural suspicion is that the truncated loss and the feature winsorisation, which make the test robust, are responsible, because the injected term (a product of two heavy-tailed standardised returns)
concentrates its variance in a few extreme days. We tested this by relaxing both (truncation level $c=6$, no winsorisation) on the same triples: power at the family-wise thresholds is still at most $0.075$ ($0.10$) at $\beta=0.5$, and the single-hypothesis power is $0.33$ and $0.38$ with the injected term carrying $54\%$ and $68\%$ of the variance (the comparison is not variance-matched), while the
rejection rate at $\beta=0$, a true null on real noise, rises to $0.075$ and $0.20$. Relaxing the robustness therefore brings no material gain at the family-wise thresholds and costs validity on real data; we could not isolate the cause of the low power; the practical consequence for interpretation is that a family-wise non-rejection does \emph{not} show that no interaction exists. Section~\ref{subsec:exp-fin} accordingly reports bounds on the average synergy of the learned forecasters.

\paragraph{Turbulence data are not a valid control.} The SURD turbulence signals (three coarse scales and the finest scale of an energy cascade) look like an ideal real-data positive control for a three-way mechanism, but they fail a placebo check. Using a null that has the same
autocorrelation as the data but is independent of the sources, phase-randomised surrogates of the finest-scale signal, both the clipped-score test and ANTE-SG ``detect'' on $20/20$ surrogates (median $\log_{10}$ final evidence $11.4$ for the clipped test, against $4.0$ on the real series;
median $\log_{10}$ running-maximum evidence $5.2$ for ANTE-SG), while ANTE-SG does not reject on the real series ($\log_{10}E_{\max}=0.95$). The signals are smooth and nearly deterministic (lag-one autocorrelation $0.9991$--$0.9995$, and $0.96$--$0.97$ after differencing) and the learned forecasters violate the prequential null in long stretches
(Remark~\ref{rem:notpop}), so these data cannot validate either procedure and we do not use them as evidence. By contrast, independent AR(1) series up to $0.999$ do not produce false rejections (Section~\ref{subsec:exp-validity}); the failure is specific to smooth, near-deterministic dynamics.
The $k$-way test (Corollary~\ref{cor:group}) is validated on synthetic data: a pure three-way mechanism $Y=0.9ABC$ or $0.5ABC$ is detected in $100/100$ replications ($[0.96,1.00]$) and a three-source additive null is not rejected ($0/100$, $[0,0.04]$).

\subsection{A fully specified search of financial data}\label{subsec:exp-fin}

\paragraph{Scope and accounting.} All data are public and listed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. A hypothesis is a target asset $C$, an unordered pair $\{A,B\}$ of other assets and a setting, tested conditionally on the market factor;
the number of hypotheses per panel and setting is $N'\binom{N'-1}{2}$ with $N'$ the number of assets other than the conditioner. The search space is written down in \texttt{experiments/revision/results/r5\_search\_space.json} before the scan ran.
It has three target types: returns, volatility ($|r|$), and stress indicators (the indicator that $|r_t|$ exceeds the expanding $90\%$ quantile of its own past); and four structures: lag $1$, lag $5$ (daily panels), weekly aggregation ($5$-day sums, lag $1$) and
a contemporaneous variant (sources at time $t$; this is labelled ``contemporaneous interaction'', not Granger prediction). There are $43$ settings (``families'') and $27{,}027$ hypotheses in total; after removing the $132$ tuples that appear identically in the ETF
and stock panels (shared assets GLD, SPY, TLT, USO, VIX) about $26{,}900$ are distinct. The search is \emph{not} pre-registered. Earlier exploratory scans of related questions (volatility, stress and crypto targets, other frequencies) preceded the grid, which was fixed before it was run but designed after those
results were known; the assets and panels were chosen by the author as a standard set, not by selecting on outcomes, and no hypothesis was dropped. The tuning constants ($\rho=0.995$, $\gamma=0.9$, warm-up $50$) were set in that earlier work on the same data, and the truncation level $c=3$ and winsorisation at $\pm5$ before this scan
but after seeing those earlier results, so the anytime-valid guarantee, which requires constants fixed independently of the series under test, holds for them only as a matter of design discipline. We therefore report each family with its own
family-wise threshold $m/\alpha$ and also a global threshold with $m=27{,}027$ ($\log_{10}(m/\alpha)=5.73$); e-values are combined by e-Bonferroni, which is valid under arbitrary dependence among the overlapping hypotheses.

hypotheses in the contemporaneous structure ($\log_{10}E=4.24$ against a threshold of $4.00$); it does not survive the global threshold. The largest running-maximum evidence in any daily or weekly returns/volatility family is $\log_{10}E=3.68$. The median final $\log_{10}E$ is $-0.24$ (a typical bet on synergy
loses about $40\%$ of its stake).

\emph{Stress indicators and hourly crypto behave differently.} For stress indicators in daily and hourly stock panels, $1588$ of $7155$ hypotheses ($22\%$) reject at the raw level and $543$ at their family-wise thresholds; for hourly crypto, $599$ of $1512$ ($40\%$) and $376$. e-BH on final e-values, pooled within each group (FDR $0.1$ or $0.05$), makes no discovery for returns and volatility, $212$ and $195$ discoveries for stress indicators and $348$ and $339$ for crypto ($513$ and $491$ pooled over all $27{,}027$ hypotheses). These counts are not evidence of synergy, for the reason given in the next paragraph: the placebo test shows that the test over-rejects on exactly these targets.

\paragraph{Null calibration on the real data.} A simulated null says little about how the test behaves on real series. We therefore calibrate on the data themselves: for a random sample of hypotheses, the target is circularly shifted by at least a year (a quarter, for weekly data) relative to
sources and conditioner, which preserves its marginal law and autocorrelation but destroys every cross-dependence, so every null is true. For daily stock volatility, daily ETF returns, hourly stock volatility and hourly stock returns ($250$, $250$, $250$ and $200$ hypotheses), the distribution of the running maximum
under the placebo coincides with the real one up to the extreme tail (Fig.~\ref{fig:placebo}); raw rejections are $2/250$ (real) versus $0/250$ (placebo) for stock volatility, $2/250$ versus $0/250$ for ETF returns, $10/250$ versus $9/250$ for hourly volatility
(placebo rate $0.036$ $[0.019,0.067]$) and $1/200$ versus $1/200$ for hourly returns. Across nine further returns-and-volatility families (lag $5$, weekly, contemporaneous and lag-$1$ variants, $150$ hypotheses each) the placebo rejects in at most $2/150$ hypotheses ($0.013$) against at most $4/150$ for the real series. The real evidence in these settings therefore sits on the placebo null, which is a more convincing calibration than comparison with a simulated null. It also explains why the median final $\log_{10}E$ is negative: with the present test the simulated null median is $-0.15$,
the placebo median $-0.20$ to $-0.26$ and the real financial median $-0.27$ ($150$ real triples). The drift concentrates in the first third of the sample (median contribution $-0.17$ for finance, $-0.11$ for the simulated null), where the forgetting learners are still estimating
the larger model, as the out-of-sample penalty of Section~\ref{subsec:cw} predicts, and is not driven by winning bets that later reverse (a running maximum above $0.3$ followed by a final value below $-0.3$ occurs for $13\%$ of finance triples and $8\%$--$11\%$ of simulated nulls).

\emph{Where the calibration fails.} The same placebo applied to the families that do reject shows that the test is not calibrated there (Fig.~\ref{fig:placebo}). With a true null, hourly crypto returns reject in $76/200$ hypotheses ($0.38$, $[0.32,0.45]$; real data $53/200$) and hourly crypto volatility in $79/200$ ($0.40$; real $108/200$);
binary stress indicators reject in $28/200$ ($0.14$) for the ETF panel, $19/200$ ($0.095$) for the stock panel and $56/200$ ($0.28$) for hourly stocks (real data: $68/200$, $56/200$ and $65/200$). For these targets the prequential null is evidently violated by the learned forecasters; we conjecture that the cause is the binary or extremely heavy-tailed nature of the series, with strong volatility clustering and a nonlinear link between the cross-product features and the target, but we have not tested it. In any case
a rejection does not indicate an $A\times B$ interaction. For daily stress indicators the real rejection rates exceed the placebo rates ($0.34$ versus $0.14$; $0.28$ versus $0.095$), which suggests some genuine cross-sectional dependence in tail events beyond the test's own bias, but we cannot separate it from a misspecified forecaster.

\paragraph{Truncation share.} For the standardised series of the search, $1.5$--$2.3\%$ of the observations lie beyond three predictable standard deviations but carry $34$--$63\%$ of the sum of squares ($36\%$--$54\%$ for daily returns and volatility, $63\%$ for hourly stock volatility), so losses truncated at $c=3$ discard a large share of the variance; this is why the loss units of Section~\ref{subsec:excl} are not untruncated $R^2$.

\paragraph{How much is excluded.} The persistent-alternative e-process of Proposition~\ref{prop:excl} is uninformative on these data, because the learners often agree to within the smallest $\delta$ on the grid ($0.0005$) and the process then wins mechanically. We therefore use the time-uniform bound on the \emph{running-average} conditional synergy of Proposition~\ref{prop:avg}
(loss units; $K=22$ grid points; level $0.05$ divided over the hypotheses of a family; Table~\ref{tab:avgbound} in Appendix~\ref{secA:bounds}). For the lag-$1$ and contemporaneous families of daily returns and volatility, the median hypothesis excludes an average synergy above $0.057$--$0.122$ loss units,
but the least well-bounded hypothesis of a family only excludes values above $0.11$--$2.15$; for lag-$5$ and weekly families, where the forecasters have more regressors or fewer observations, the median bound is $0.22$--$0.75$ and the worst case is $0.77$--$1.91$ (weekly) and $6.4$--$19.6$ (lag $5$). The realised mean score is negative in every family (median $-0.005$ to $-0.034$ per step), as the out-of-sample penalty predicts.
Because the bound uses the worst-case variance proxy $b_s^2$, it is conservative, and it is weak against magnitudes that are plausible for daily returns (a gain of order $1\%$ of the variance): for a typical hypothesis the data exclude average synergies above roughly $6$--$12\%$ of the (truncated) target variance per step, while the simultaneous guarantee for a whole family is the maximum bound ($0.11$--$2.15$ at lag $1$ and contemporaneous), and neither excludes smaller effects.

\paragraph{Crises.} Running e-processes are plotted across whole families in Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}, so that evidence that rises in a crisis and then decays is visible, which a final-value summary would hide. In the $660$ stock-volatility and $1365$ ETF-return families the maximum over hypotheses of $\log_{10}E_t$ never approaches the family-wise thresholds ($4.1$ and $4.4$): its peak is $1.59$ (January 2017) and $2.20$ (5 February 2020, \emph{before} the COVID sell-off, after which it decays), and during the three windows examined (the 2018 volatility spike, February--June 2020, and 2022) the median over hypotheses of the within-window maximum is between $-0.10$ and $-0.22$, with only $1.2\%$--$2.2\%$ of hypotheses exceeding $\log_{10}E=0.3$ at any time in a window. The cross-sectional median drifts to about $-0.26$ to $-0.30$ and the $95$th percentile stays at zero (the bet is switched off).

\paragraph{Truncation level and winsorisation.} For $200$ random hypotheses from each of daily stock volatility and daily ETF returns, varying the truncation level ($c=2,3,5$) and removing the feature winsorisation changes the raw rejection count by at most two hypotheses ($1$--$2/200$ at $c=2$, $0/200$ for $c=5$ or without winsorisation,
$1/200$ at the default) and the median final $\log_{10}E$ stays between $-0.15$ and $-0.33$; the conclusions for daily returns and volatility are therefore not sensitive to these choices.

\paragraph{Forgetting factor and the Clark--West diagnostic.} With forgetting factors from $0.98$ to $0.999$ the false-rejection rate is at most $0.010$ in every simulated null cell ($100$ replications each; Wilson upper limits up to $0.054$) and the real-data rejection rate at most $0.047$ ($150$ financial triples, $\rho=0.98$), and the median final
$\log_{10}E$ stays between $-0.04$ and $-0.27$; $\rho=0.98$ makes the learners unstable (the Clark--West adjustment term explodes) and is not recommended. The Clark--West-adjusted mean score is $0.03$--$0.13$ for $\rho\in[0.99,0.999]$ on both simulated and financial data,
which gives the size of the noise floor; as stated in Section~\ref{subsec:cw} we do not use it for inference.

\paragraph{The pairwise layer.} \citet{billio2012} measure connectedness through pairwise Granger networks. On the same panels, the fraction of significant ordered pairs (Bonferroni at $0.05$) is $0.20$ for ETF returns and $0.78$ for ETF volatility at lag $1$
($0.10$ and $0.61$ for stocks), and a rolling $252$-day window (raw $5\%$ level) gives a density of up to $0.55$ (ETFs) and $0.40$ (stocks) around the 2020 crash against a median of about $0.1$. Daily markets therefore have a strong pairwise layer, particularly in volatility. For $660$ stock-volatility triples, $145$ have both single-source tests
significant after conditioning on SPY ($29$ after Bonferroni), but only $4$ reach a raw ANTE-SG rejection and none a family-wise one: the pairwise structure is not accompanied by detectable super-additive structure.

\subsection{Interpretation}\label{subsec:exp-interp}

We summarise what the evidence supports, keeping the wording to the searched space. In daily and weekly returns and volatility of the panels examined, for lag $1$, lag $5$, weekly and contemporaneous structures, the ANTE-SG test finds no statistically significant super-additive predictive group effect
at the family-wise level, and the real-data evidence has the distribution of a placebo null. Where significant evidence does appear (stress indicators; hourly crypto), a placebo test shows that the test rejects a true null in $10$--$40\%$ of hypotheses for these targets, so the rejections are not interpretable as synergy and the test would need a different forecaster or score (binary or heavy-tailed targets) to be usable there;
We do not claim that synergy is absent from financial markets in general, and not even from the settings searched, for three reasons developed above: the test has low power on real financial noise even for large injected interactions (semi-synthetic power), the predictor class limits what can be detected,
and non-rejection is a statement about these online forecasters (Remark~\ref{rem:notpop}).

\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{sec:discussion}

ANTE reframes synergy detection from estimation to \emph{testing} and from fixed-sample to \emph{anytime-valid} inference. The methodological result is a pair of tests with time-uniform error control: an interventional test under a known randomised design
whose null is sequential and therefore allows nonstationarity, and a predictive test for time series whose null compares two learned forecasters and whose validity proof is exact (Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid}) once the score is built from a truncated loss and
a bound that is known before each observation. The cost is low power on real financial noise, which we quantified by injection (Section~\ref{subsec:exp-pv}) and whose cause we could not isolate (relaxing the truncation does not cure it); a second cost, common to every
anytime-valid procedure, is lower power than an oracle fixed-sample test at a single pre-committed sample size.

\paragraph{Arguments that do not hold.} Three tempting arguments are avoided here. (i) A validity proof for the group score by clipping fails, because clipping does not in general preserve the sign of the conditional mean
(Example~\ref{ex:clipfail}). (ii) Isolation of an interaction from joint dependence does not follow from additivity of the mechanism: it needs orthogonality, which holds under independence or span conditions and can fail when sources are dependent and the mechanism lies outside the feature span (Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}),
and the four-model contrast is contaminated by redundancy and suppression. (iii) A real-data positive or negative control is only informative if the test has been calibrated against a placebo null on the same kind of data: smooth near-deterministic signals (turbulence), binary stress indicators and hourly crypto series show that the test can reject a true null there.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1) A rejection means that the joint forecaster had a persistently positive conditional advantage over the additive one (Remark~\ref{rem:notpop}); reading it as an irreducible $A\times B$ interaction needs the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}, which cannot be checked in data, and reading it as causal needs an
interventional design. (2) The prequential null is not implied by zero population synergy, so Type-I control for population-level nulls is established by simulation and placebo calibration, not by theorem; the simulations include heavy tails, GARCH, nonstationarity and correlated sources but are not exhaustive, and the test fails on smooth, near-deterministic signals such as the turbulence data.
(3) Power: the test has low power for lagged interactions between heavy-tailed returns (which relaxing the truncation does not cure), loses power as the two sources become nearly collinear (the interaction is then not identifiable), and its detectable alternatives depend on the dictionary; a random-feature dictionary removes one blind spot but the choice of dictionary is fixed in advance. For the implemented plug-in bet we prove validity only.
(4) Families are not pre-registered and overlap (shared assets, repeated targets), so the e-Bonferroni thresholds, though valid under arbitrary dependence, are conservative. (5) Conclusions are specific to the panels, targets, lag structures and sample periods searched; for hourly crypto data and binary stress indicators the test is not calibrated (Section~\ref{subsec:exp-fin}), so the significant evidence there cannot be interpreted. (6) The augmented (AIPW) interventional score with estimated propensities is an extension without a finite-sample guarantee.

\paragraph{Future work.} Kernel or neural forecasters in the same betting scaffold, together with the sequential kernelised tests of \citet{podkopaev2023}; anytime-valid conditional-independence tests \citep{grunwald2024ci} to condition on confounders and move from predictive to causal readings;
a sequential comparison with regret guarantees for the plug-in bet; and an information-theoretic instantiation in which a permutation-recentred PEID increment is bet on.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} We introduced ANTE, an anytime-valid sequential test of group synergy with an interventional and a predictive formulation. It controls Type-I error uniformly over all stopping times for the sequential null of the interventional part and for the forecaster-comparison null of the predictive part (Theorems~\ref{thm:validity} and~\ref{thm:sgvalid}); for population-level nulls in real data, control is established only by simulation and placebo calibration. The test has power one and a high-probability detection-time bound for an oracle bet (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}, Proposition~\ref{prop:latency}), extends to $k$-way groups (Corollary~\ref{cor:group}), and comes with an exclusion e-process, a time-uniform bound on average synergy and a precise account of what a rejection means (Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}).
Empirically it holds its level under continuous monitoring and under a range of simulated stress nulls and, on real data, in daily returns and volatility (but not for binary stress indicators or hourly crypto, where it is miscalibrated), and in a fully specified search of financial data it finds no statistically significant super-additive predictive group effect in daily and weekly returns and volatility, with real-data evidence that matches a placebo null. The methodological result and the empirical finding are separate:
the first is a theorem and simulation record; the second is a finding about the specific panels searched.

\section{Proofs and supporting results}\label{secA:proofs}

Throughout, ``predictable'' means $\F_{t-1}$-measurable, $\gamma<1$, and Ville's inequality is
used in the form: if $(M_t)_{t\ge0}$ is a nonnegative supermartingale with $\E M_0\le1$ then
$\Prob(\exists t:M_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$.

\subsection*{A.1 Proofs for Part A}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}]
Write $\psi_t=\sum_{a,b}c(a,b)\indic\{A_t=a,B_t=b\}C_t(a,b)/\pi_t(a,b)$. Condition on
$(\mathcal H_t,\{C_t(\cdot)\})$. By sequential randomisation each term has conditional mean
$c(a,b)C_t(a,b)$, because $\pi_t$ is $\mathcal H_t$-measurable. Taking $\E[\cdot\mid\mathcal H_t]$ gives
$\theta_t(X_t)$, and the tower property gives $\E[\psi_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\theta_t$. The bound follows from
$0\le C_t\le1$ and positivity.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:validity} and Corollary~\ref{cor:anytime}]
Each factor $1+\lambda_t\psi_t\ge1-\gamma>0$, so $E_t\in(0,(1+\gamma)^t]$ is integrable. Since $\lambda_t$ is predictable,
$\E[E_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=E_{t-1}(1+\lambda_t\theta_t)$. Under $H_0^{\le}$ and $\lambda_t\ge0$ this is at
most $E_{t-1}$. Under $H_0^{=}$ it equals $E_{t-1}$, and an average of two martingales for the
same filtration is a martingale. Ville's inequality gives the crossing bound, and optional stopping
for nonnegative supermartingales gives $\E E_\tau\le1$ for every stopping time.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:log}]
Put $q(x)=(x-\log(1+x))/x^2=\int_0^1v/(1+xv)\,dv$ for $x>-1$; $q$ is strictly decreasing, so
$q(x)\le q(-\gamma)=C_\gamma$ on $[-\gamma,\infty)$, with equality at $-\gamma$. Finally
$q(-\gamma)=\int_0^1v/(1-\gamma v)dv\le1/(2(1-\gamma))$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}]
Let $W_s=\log(1+\lambda\psi_s)$. Since $|\lambda\psi_s|\le\bar\gamma$, Lemma~\ref{lem:log} gives
$\E[W_s\mid\F_{s-1}]\ge\lambda\delta-C_{\bar\gamma}\lambda^2c^2=g_0$. The differences
$D_s=W_s-\E[W_s\mid\F_{s-1}]$ are martingale differences bounded by
$\ell_\lambda=\log\frac{1+\lambda c}{1-\lambda c}$, so $\sum_s\E D_s^2/s^2<\infty$ and the martingale
strong law gives $t^{-1}\sum_{s\le t}D_s\to0$ a.s. Hence
$\liminf t^{-1}\log E_t\ge g_0$ a.s. The statement for the cap-based Kelly bet follows by
substituting $\lambda^\star=\delta(1-\gamma)/c^2$ and the bound $C_{\bar\gamma}\le1/(2(1-\gamma))$.
(Almost-sure \emph{convergence} of $t^{-1}\log E_t$ would need an ergodicity assumption; the
$\liminf$ statement does not.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:latency}]
$\{\tau_\alpha>t\}\subseteq\{\log E_t<\log(1/\alpha)\}$ and $\log E_t\ge tg_0+M_t$ with
$M_t=\sum_{s\le t}D_s$. Each $D_s$ lies in a predictable interval of length $\ell_\lambda$, so
Azuma--Hoeffding \citep{azuma1967} gives $\Prob(M_t\le-\epsilon)\le\exp(-2\epsilon^2/(t\ell_\lambda^2))$;
take $\epsilon=tg_0-\log(1/\alpha)>0$. For $t\ge2\log(1/\alpha)/g_0$ the exponent is at least
$tg_0^2/(2\ell_\lambda^2)$, which exceeds $\log(1/\beta)$ once $t\ge2\ell_\lambda^2\log(1/\beta)/g_0^2$.
(Using the range at the cap, $\log\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}$, in place of $\ell_\lambda$ is valid but
gives a bound of order $\delta^{-4}$.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:mixture}]
An average of supermartingales for one filtration is a supermartingale; a sum of nonnegative
terms is at least its largest term, so $E^{\mathrm{mix}}_t\ge K^{-1}\max_jE_t(\lambda_j)$. On the geometric grid,
$g_0$ is a concave quadratic with maximiser $\lambda^\star$, so some grid point in
$[\lambda^\star/2,\lambda^\star]$ has $g_0\ge\tfrac34g_0(\lambda^\star)$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Known-design AIPW.} Conditioning as in Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}, the weighted residual term
of $\phi_t$ has conditional mean $\sum c(a,b)(\E[C_t(a,b)\mid\mathcal H_t]-\hat\mu_{ab,t})$ and the
$\hat\mu$ terms cancel, so $\E[\phi_t\mid\mathcal H_t]=\theta_t(X_t)$ for any $\F_{t-1}$-measurable $\hat\mu$.
One indicator is nonzero, contributing at most $1/\pi_{\min}$, and $|\sum c\,\hat\mu|\le2$, so
$|\phi_t|\le1/\pi_{\min}+2$.

\subsection*{A.2 Proofs for Part B}

\paragraph{Why the clipped score fails (Example~\ref{ex:clipfail}).} Let $s_t$ be i.i.d.\ with the law in the
example and bet a fixed $\lambda=0.1$ on $u_t=\clip(s_t,-5,5)$. Then
$t^{-1}\log E_t\to0.1\log(1-5\lambda)+0.9\log(1+1.5\lambda)=0.0565>0$ a.s., so $E_t\to\infty$ although
$\E s_t=-0.65$. The plug-in bet behaves identically: $\hat\mu/\hat v\to0.188$ exceeds the cap $0.1$, so $\lambda_t=0.1$
eventually. A data-dependent scale does not repair this: $\Prob(s=-100)=0.01$, $\Prob(s=1)=0.99$ has $\E s=-0.01$, root
mean square $10.05$ and, with $b=5$, $\E\,\clip(s/\mathrm{RMS},-5,5)=+0.0485$; and
$\Prob(s=-1000)=0.001$, $\Prob(s=0.99)=0.999$ with $b=10$ has $\E s=-0.011$, RMS $31.6$ and clipped mean $+0.0213$.
Two sufficient conditions do exist. Writing $m_t=\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]$, $\sigma$ for the scale and
$U_t=\E[(s_t-b\sigma)_+\mid\F_{t-1}]$, $L_t=\E[(-s_t-b\sigma)_+\mid\F_{t-1}]$, one has the exact identity
$\sigma\,\E[\clip(s_t/\sigma,-b,b)\mid\F_{t-1}]=m_t-U_t+L_t$, so the raw null implies the clipped null if and
only if $L_t\le U_t-m_t$. In particular, if the conditional law of $s_t$ is symmetric about $m_t$, then $m_t\le0$ implies
the clipped mean is $\le0$ for every predictable $\sigma>0$ and $b>0$ (the map $x\mapsto\int\clip(x+z)Q(dz)$ is nondecreasing and
vanishes at $0$ for symmetric $Q$). The null stated on the clipped score itself is always valid
(supermartingale by the argument below), but it is not the scientific null.

\begin{lemma}[Predictable bound]\label{lem:bound}
For $\ell_c(e)=\min(e^2,c^2)$ and any $y,p^1,p^2$,
$|\ell_c(y-p^1)-\ell_c(y-p^2)|\le c^2-(c-|p^1-p^2|)_+^2\le\min\{2c|p^1-p^2|,c^2\}$, and the first bound is attained.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\ell_c(e)=h(|e|)$ with $h(r)=\min(r^2,c^2)$, which is $2c$-Lipschitz, and $||e|-|e'||\le|e-e'|$; this gives the
$2c|p^1-p^2|$ bound. For the sharp bound take $r_1=|y-p^1|\le r_2=|y-p^2|\le r_1+d$ with $d=|p^1-p^2|$; the difference
$\min(r_2,c)^2-\min(r_1,c)^2$ is at most $\min(r_1+d,c)^2-\min(r_1,c)^2$, which is maximised over $r_1$
at $c^2-(c-d)_+^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sgvalid} and Corollary~\ref{cor:group}]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:bound}, $|s_t|\le b_t$ for every outcome, so $|u_t|\le1$ and $s_t$ is integrable. Because $b_t$ and
$\indic\{b_t>\varepsilon\}$ are predictable (functions of predictions made before $Y_t$ is seen),
$\E[u_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\indic\{b_t>\varepsilon\}\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]/b_t\le0$ under $H_0^{\mathrm{preq}}$.
With $\lambda_t\in[0,\gamma]$ predictable, $1+\lambda_tu_t\ge1-\gamma>0$ and
$\E[E_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=E_{t-1}(1+\lambda_t\E[u_t\mid\F_{t-1}])\le E_{t-1}$. Ville's inequality applies. For the capped
process, $x\mapsto\min(x,C)$ is concave, so by Jensen $\E[E'_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\le\min\{E'_{t-1}(1+\lambda_t\E[u_t\mid\F_{t-1}]),C\}\le E'_{t-1}$.
For contemporaneous features, replace $\F_{t-1}$ by $\mathcal G_t$ in the conditional mean of $u_t$ and use the tower property:
$\E[E_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\E[E_{t-1}(1+\lambda_t\E[u_t\mid\mathcal G_t])\mid\F_{t-1}]\le E_{t-1}$. The group case only changes the feature maps.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:isolation}]
Let $\Pi_S$ denote orthogonal projection onto the span of feature set $S$, $r_{\mathrm{add}}=Y-\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}Y$ and $\tilde X=X-\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}X$
for the cross-product block $X$.
(a) The joint span is the orthogonal sum of the additive span and $\mathrm{span}(\tilde X)$, so by Pythagoras
$\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=\|\Pi_{\tilde X}r_{\mathrm{add}}\|^2=\kappa^\top\Sigma_{\tilde X}^{+}\kappa\ge0$ with $\kappa=\E[Xr_{\mathrm{add}}]$,
which is zero if and only if $\kappa=0$.
(b) The identity $\Syn_4=\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}-R$ with $R=[L(\mathrm{base})-L(A)]-[L(B)-L(\mathrm{add})]$ is algebra; if the centred blocks
$\bar F_A,\bar F_B$ are orthogonal then $\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}=\Pi_W+\Pi_{\bar F_A}+\Pi_{\bar F_B}$ and $R=0$. In the Gaussian example with $Y=A\pm B+\varepsilon$ and $\mathrm{corr}(A,B)=\rho$:
$L(\mathrm{add})=L(\mathrm{joint})=\sigma^2$, $L(A)=L(B)=1-\rho^2+\sigma^2$ and $L(\mathrm{base})=2\pm2\rho+\sigma^2$, which gives
$\Syn_4=-2\rho(1+\rho)$ for $+$ and $+2\rho(1-\rho)$ for $-$.
(c) (S1): $Y-\varepsilon\in\mathrm{span}(\mathrm{add})$, so $r_{\mathrm{add}}=\varepsilon$ and $\E[X\varepsilon]=0$. (S2): by independence, centred own-block spans are mutually orthogonal, so
$r_{\mathrm{add}}=r_m(W)+r_f(\mathcal A)+r_h(\mathcal B)+\varepsilon$ with each term centred and orthogonal to its own block, and every
product $\E[A_\ell B_\ell\,r_\bullet]$ factorises to zero. (S3): after standardisation expand $f=\sum a_kHe_k(A)$, $h=\sum b_kHe_k(B)$ in Hermite
polynomials; by Mehler's identity $He_k(A)$ ($k\ge3$) is orthogonal to the additive span, so
$r_{\mathrm{add}}=\sum_{k\ge3}(a_kHe_k(A)+b_kHe_k(B))+\varepsilon$; and $\E[AB\,He_k(A)]=\rho\,\E[A^2He_k(A)]=0$ for $k\ge3$ since $A^2=He_2+He_0$.
(d) For $B=A^2+\tau V$, $Y=A^3+\varepsilon$: the features $1,A^2,B,B^2$ are even in $A$ and $A$, $X=AB$ odd, so $r_{\mathrm{add}}=He_3(A)+\varepsilon$ and
$\tilde X=He_3(A)+\tau AV$, giving $\kappa=6$, $\E\tilde X^2=6+\tau^2$ and $\Syn_{\mathrm{aj}}=36/(6+\tau^2)$. For the Gaussian two-lag example,
$X=A_{t-1}B_{t-1}=A_{t-1}A_{t-2}+vA_{t-1}V_{t-1}$ is orthogonal to the additive span with $\E[XY]=1$ and $\E X^2=1+v^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:excl}]
Since $|s_t|\le b_t$ and $\delta>0$, $w_t\in[(\delta-b_t)/(\delta+b_t),1]\subset(-1,1]$, so every factor is at least $1-\gamma$, and
$\E[w_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=(\delta-\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}])/(b_t+\delta)\le0$ under $H_\delta$ because $b_t+\delta>0$ is predictable. Hence $K(\delta)$ is a supermartingale and Ville's inequality applies;
a union bound over the grid gives the second statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:avg}]
Because $s_s\in[-b_s,b_s]$ with $b_s$ predictable, Hoeffding's lemma applied conditionally gives
$\E[\exp(\lambda(m_s-s_s))\mid\F_{s-1}]\le\exp(\lambda^2b_s^2/2)$. Hence
$Z_t(\lambda)=\exp\bigl(\lambda\sum_{s=t_1}^t(m_s-s_s)-\tfrac{\lambda^2}2V_t\bigr)$ is a nonnegative supermartingale with $Z_{t_1-1}=1$, and
Ville's inequality gives $\Prob(\exists t:Z_t(\lambda_j)\ge K/\alpha)\le\alpha/K$. A union bound over $j$ and rearranging $Z_t(\lambda_j)<K/\alpha$ gives the stated bound; the lower bound follows from $-s_s$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Estimation noise and Clark--West.} With squared loss, $\E[s_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=(\mu_t-a_t)^2-(\mu_t-j_t)^2$ where
$a_t,j_t$ are the two forecasts and $\mu_t=\E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]$. The Clark--West adjusted score $s_t+(a_t-j_t)^2$ has conditional mean $2(\mu_t-a_t)(j_t-a_t)$, which is zero only
when $a_t=\mu_t$ and can otherwise have either sign; it is therefore a diagnostic and not a valid centred score.
